About me

A little about me

I was born and raised in Port Antonio, Portland, where I attended Titchfield High School. My passion for communication led me to the University of Technology, Jamaica (UTech), where I earned a bachelor's degree in Communication Arts and Technology, majoring in Journalism.

Journalism taught me how powerful clear, confident communication can be. Today, I bring that background into the English Language and Literature classroom. My goal is to help every student find their voice and express themselves effectively, whether they are writing an essay, analysing a poem, or speaking in front of their peers.

I teach English Language and Literature to students in grades 7 to 9 at Happy Grove High School in Portland. I am also completing my Postgraduate Diploma in Education at The College of Agriculture, Science and Education (CASE) in Portland, as I continue to grow as an educator.

Philosophy of teaching

What I believe about learning

“In my classroom, students do the thinking, find their voices, and feel respected enough to speak up.”

I believe that every student learns differently, so no single method can reach every learner. For this reason, I use differentiated instruction in my English Language and Literature classes. In one lesson, students may discuss a text, read independently, complete a hands-on activity, or play a learning game. This variety gives every student, whatever their strengths, a way into the lesson.

I see my role as a facilitator rather than a lecturer. I keep my classroom student-centred, so my students do most of the thinking and the hands-on work while I guide them, ask questions, and make sure they stay on the right track. I move away from the "banking" approach, where the teacher simply deposits information into students. Instead, I want my students to think critically, build their own understanding, and leave my class able to express themselves and communicate effectively.

Respect is the foundation of my classroom. I respect my students' feelings, and in return I expect them to respect the classroom, their classmates, and me. I am assertive in my classroom management, and I do not tolerate bullying. As a result, my students feel free to speak up, ask questions, and ask for correction without fear. The atmosphere is calm and settled, ready for learning, with room to share a laugh along the way.

To know where my students are, I assess them in many ways. I walk the classroom as students work, ask questions throughout the lesson, and use exit tickets at the end. Students also show their learning through presentations, activities, online quizzes, and lessons turned into games. What I learn from these checks shapes what I teach next.

Week 8 · Grade 8³
Nov 2, 2026

Bringing poetry to life with "Linstead Market"

What happened. I introduced my Grade 8³ class to poetry, focusing on the structure of ballads and free verse. We began by clapping the beat of the Jamaican folk song "Linstead Market", then compared it with a passage from My Father, Sun-Sun Johnson to hear the difference between a regular beat and natural speech. Students then worked in pairs to mark the stanzas and rhymes in "The Wreck of the Hesperus" and "Fog", completed a comparison matrix, checked another pair's work, and finished with an exit ticket.

How I felt. This lesson was fun to teach. The energy in the room was great, and it was rewarding to see the class so involved in their first poetry lesson of the term.

What went well. Students enjoyed clapping to "Linstead Market" the most. Starting with a song they already knew made the idea of meter easy to grasp and connected poetry to their own culture. They also responded well to marking the poems in pairs, because bracketing stanzas, circling end words, and joining rhymes kept them actively involved. The exit tickets showed that most students could identify a stanza, label an ABCB rhyme scheme, and explain the difference between a ballad and free verse.

What didn't go as well. Some pairs struggled to work together, and in those pairs one partner did most of the work while the other watched.

What I learned. Music is a powerful way into poetry for my students. When a lesson connects to something familiar and lets them move and make sound, they engage more and understand faster. This supports my belief in differentiation and Gardner's idea that students learn through different intelligences, including musical intelligence. I also learned that pair work needs clear structure so that every student takes part.

What I'll do next time. I will bring more music and songs into my poetry lessons, for example by inviting students to share Jamaican songs that tell a story and mapping their rhyme schemes together. I will also check in with pairs early and make sure partners swap roles, so the work is shared fairly.

Week 5 · Grade 8³
Oct 5, 2026

Two goals, one chair: teaching conflict

What happened. This lesson introduced external conflict (Man vs. Man) through the rivalry between Sun-Sun and Jake Hibbertson in Chapters 5 and 6 of My Father, Sun-Sun Johnson. We opened with a short demonstration in which two students tried to use one chair for completely different goals, which led into a discussion of what conflict really is. Students then worked in pairs to underline Sun-Sun's actions and circle Jake's obstacles in the text, built a conflict matrix together on the board, completed a conflict map, checked another pair's work, and finished with an exit ticket.

How I felt. I was pleased with this lesson. The class stayed focused, and students did the thinking while I guided them, which is exactly how I want my classroom to work.

What went well. The lesson went well from start to finish. The chair demonstration gave students a clear, concrete picture of two goals that cannot both be met before we turned to the novel. Because students found and presented the evidence themselves, they built their own understanding of how Jake acts as a barrier to Sun-Sun. The exit tickets showed that almost everyone could define Man vs. Man conflict, identify one of Sun-Sun's goals, and name an action Jake takes against it.

What didn't go as well. Although almost every student did well, a small number still needed extra support to explain how the conflict drives the plot.

What I learned. A simple, concrete demonstration can unlock an abstract literary idea. I also saw the value of scaffolding: sentence frames such as "Sun-Sun wants to ____ but Jake prevents this by ____" gave students the support they needed to reach the objective on their own, as Vygotsky describes.

What I'll do next time. I will keep the demonstration and the student-led evidence search. I will also identify the students who need support earlier, give them the sentence frame from the start, and follow up with them in the next lesson.

About this website

Why I made this site

Purpose

This e-portfolio was created as part of my Postgraduate Diploma in Education at The College of Agriculture, Science and Education (CASE). It brings together evidence of my growth as a teacher, from my philosophy and reflections to the work I've done with my students.

As an English Language and Literature teacher, I believe students learn best when they can see their own voices and experiences reflected in what they read and write. This site is my way of showing that belief in action, through the lessons I plan, the tools I use, and the work my students produce.

It is a living portfolio. I will keep adding to it as I gain experience, try new strategies, and grow as an educator.

How to use it

Use the menu at the top to move between pages, or start from the cards on the home page. Each page covers one part of my practice.

How it was built

Designed and coded in HTML and CSS with the help of AI tools. All writing, reflections, and classroom work are my own.
